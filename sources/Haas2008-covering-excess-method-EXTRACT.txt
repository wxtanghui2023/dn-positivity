Chapter 1
Introduction
Let K(n, R) denote the minimal cardinality of a binary code of length n and
covering radius R. This thesis is concerned with lower bounds on K(n, R)
by the method of covering excess, which was ﬁrst proposed by Johnson [18]
in 1972 and later rediscovered by van Wee [29] in 1988.
1.1 A sphere-covering problem
Let F = {0, 1} denote the ﬁnite ﬁeld with two elements. To measure the
similarity between two words of length n over F, we introduce their Hamming
distance, which simply counts the number of places, where the words diﬀer.
The space Fn of the binary words of length n over F, equipped with the
Hamming distance, is one of the most important ﬁnite metric spaces.
Like in every (ﬁnite) metric space there are two fundamental questions,
the sphere-packing and the sphere-covering problem.
(i) Determine the maximal number A(n, 2e+1) of non-overlapping spheres
of given radius e, that can be placed in the Hamming space Fn.
(ii) Determine the minimal number K(n, R) of spheres of given radius R
needed to cover the whole space Fn.
The quantity A(n, d) was widely studied during the last six decades in the
framework of error-correcting codes. The sphere-covering problem (ii) how-
ever was not studied equally extensive, but in the last two or three decades,
it attracted much attention to the mathematicians. In generally the deter-
mination of K(n, R) is very diﬃcult and we have to be content with lower
3
and upper bounds. A trivial lower bound for K(n, R) certainly is the cardi-
nality of the whole space Fn divided by the cardinality of a Hamming sphere
with radius R. Many methods are known to improve on this lower bound,
the so-called sphere-covering bound. One of the most eﬃcient method is the
covering excess counting method, which is the object of the present thesis.
It relies on the fact, that for arithmetical reasons under certain conditions
a sphere-covering is forced to cover words in a small Hamming sphere more
than once, the so-called covering excess.
1.2 Overview of the thesis
In chapter 2 we give the necessary background on codes as well as the no-
tations and terminology on covering excess, which are used throughout this
work. As a ﬁrst instance of the covering excess counting method we give
Johnson’s and van Wee’s bound on K(n, 1) for even n, which makes use of a
forced covering excess in a sphere of radius one.
In chapter 3 we study bounds for K(n, 1) when n is odd. We ﬁrst use a
method of Blass and Litsyn [1] to give the presently best known lower bound
for K(n, 1) if n ≡ 5 (mod 6). Then we discuss the general excess bound,
which follows from the fact, that if n ≡ − 1 (mod p) for an odd prime p,
there exists a covering excess on a sphere with radius p − 1. We give a new
lower bound for the covering excess, leading to a ”general improvement of
the general excess bound” for binary codes with covering radius one.
In chapter 4 we study bounds for K(n, R), which rely on a covering excess
in spheres with radius one. We describe a classical bound of van Wee for
this case from 1988 and a slight improvement of this bound by Honkala. We
present a short proof of a further improvement of Honkala’s bound. Moreover
we give an improvement of the van Wee bound in case that R + 1 divides n,
when both bounds coincide and no general further progress of the van Wee
bound is known up to now.

Like in every (ﬁnite) metric space there exists the fundamental sphere-

(i) Determine the maximal number of non-overlapping spheres of given
radius e, that can be placed in the Hamming space Fn.
One easily sees, that the centers of the spheres in such a packing form a code
C ⊂ Fn with minimum distance at least 2 e + 1. Thus the number requested
in (i) is A(n, 2e + 1), where A(n, d) denotes the maximal cardinality of a code
C ⊂ Fn with minimum distance at least d.
Assume that a set of messages is encoded by the words of a code C ⊂ Fn
with minimum distance at least 2 e + 1. If during the transmission of such
a word there occur at most e errors, then these errors can be corrected by
the receiver. Thus A(n, d) is the most basic quantity in coding theory, since
A(n, 2e + 1) is the maximal cardinality of a code correcting up to e errors.
For a monograph on error-correcting codes see [26]. Updated internet tables
for A(n, d) are given by Brouwer [2].
In this thesis however we are concerned with the dual sphere-covering
problem:
(ii) Determine the minimal number of spheres of given radius R needed to
cover the whole space Fn.
The covering radius R = R(C) of a code C ⊂ Fn denotes the least integer
R, such that the spheres with radius R centered on the codewords cover
the whole space. It is clear, that the centers of the spheres of a covering
described in (ii) form a code with covering radius at most R. Equivalently,
a code C ⊂ Fn has covering radius (at most) R, if
∀x ∈ Fn ∃c ∈ C with d(x, c) ≤ R (2.2)
holds or, simply, if each x ∈ Fn is R-covered by C. K(n, R) denotes the
minimal cardinality of a code C ⊂ Fn with covering radius (at most) R.

codeword covers always an even number of points in a Hamming sphere, but
the cardinality of the sphere is odd. Then a covering excess in that sphere is
unavoidable.
The ﬁrst who introduced the method of covering excess was Johnson [18]
in 1972, but his remarkable paper remained unnoticed until the appearance
of a monograph on covering codes [4] in 1997. The method was rediscovered
by van Wee [29] in 1988, so it should be quite fair, to attribute the method
both to Johnson and van Wee. After some prerequisites in section 2.2 we
recall their most prominent example in section 2.3, a lower bound for K(n, 1)
for even n.

Chapter 3
Bounds for K(n, 1) with odd n
In this chapter we study bounds for K(n, 1), where n is odd. We start with
the case n ≡ 5 (mod 6), where a covering excess in a sphere of radius two
can be found. We describe a classical bound of van Wee [29] for this case
as well as an improvement due to Blass and Litsyn [1]. We further improve
on this bound, giving the presently best known lower bound for K(n, 1) if
n ≡ 5 (mod 6).
Next we discuss the general excess bound for K(n, 1) (due to Habsieger
[9] and Honkala [16]), which follows from the fact, that if n ≡ − 1 (mod p)
for an odd prime p, there exists a covering excess ≥ p − 1 on a sphere with
radius p − 1. Answering a question of Habsieger, we prove that this covering
excess indeed grows quadratic in p. This yields a ”general improvement of
the general excess bound” for binary codes with covering radius one. The
proof uses a classiﬁcation theorem for certain subset systems as well as new
congruence properties for the excess function, which were conjectured by
Habsieger.

3.2 The case n ≡ 1, 3 ( mod 6)
We have seen, that in the case n ≡ 5 (mod 6) a binary code of length n
and covering radius one forces a covering excess in every sphere of radius
two (Theorem 5). In this section we have a look on the remaining odd cases
n ≡ 1, 3 (mod 6) and show, that then there are coverings of spheres with
radius three indeed without covering excess. For the proof we have to review
shortly some basic facts on Steiner systems.
3.2.1 Steiner systems
We shortly give the deﬁnition and a few basic facts for Steiner systems, which
are basic objects in design theory. They are related to the construction of
perfect coverings of Hamming spheres, i.e. covering codes without covering
excess on certain spheres.
Deﬁnition 17. A Steiner system S(t, k, n) is a collection of k-subsets (called
blocks) of {1, . . . , n}, such that every t-subset of {1, . . . , n} is contained in
exactly one of the blocks.
The connection to coding theory is given by the quantity supp deﬁned in
(2.3), which yields a natural one to one correspondence between the words
x ∈ Fn and the subsets of {1, . . . , n}.
The history of Steiner systems goes back until 1847, when Kirkman con-
sidered the special case t = 2 and k = 3. A Steiner system S(2, 3, n) is called
a Steiner triple system.
Theorem 18 (Kirkman [20] 1847) . A Steiner triple system S(2, 3, n) exists
if and only if n ≡ 1, 3 ( mod 6).
A Steiner system S(3, 4, n) is called a Steiner quadrupel system.
Theorem 19 (Hanani [12] 1960) . A Steiner quadrupel system S(3, 4, n) ex-
ists if and only if n ≡ 2, 4 ( mod 6).
25
3.2.2 Perfect coverings of spheres
Deﬁnition 20. A perfect R-covering of a Hamming sphere B(x, r) with x ∈
Fn is a code C ⊂ Fn, such that each y ∈ B(x, r) is R-covered exactly once by
C.
Clearly a perfect code C ⊂ Fn with covering radius R is a perfect R-
covering of any sphere B(x, r), x ∈ Fn, r ≥ 0. Perfect coverings of spheres
are related to Steiner systems as we will show in the next theorem.
Theorem 21. A perfect 1-covering of a Hamming sphere B ⊂ Fn with radius
three exists, if and only if there exists a Steiner quadruple system S(3, 4, n +
1), i.e. if n ≡ 1, 3 ( mod 6).
Proof. It suﬃces to consider the case B = B(0, 3) with the all-zero word
0 ∈ Fn.
Assume D1, . . . , Dr ⊂ {1, . . . , n+ 1} are the blocks of a Steiner quadruple
system S(3, 4, n + 1) on the set {1, . . . , n + 1}. Then one easily sees, that
the code C = {0, supp−1(D1 \ {n + 1}), . . . , supp−1(Dr \ {n + 1})} ⊂ Fn is a
perfect 1-covering of B(0, 3).
Conversely assume that C ⊂ Fn is a perfect 1-covering of B(0, 3). Let
Ci ⊂ C be the words of weight i in C, i ≥ 0. If 0 ∈ C, then C1 = C2 = ∅. One

3.4 Habsiegers improvement and the excess

Let C ⊂ Fn be a code with covering radius one, n odd. Theorem 24 states,

covering excess in a sphere with radius p − 1. This gives rise to the natural
question, under which circumstances there exists a perfect 1-covering of a

with radius p − 2, where n is odd and p is the least odd prime divisor of n + 1
cannot proved or refuted when p ≥ 7, see section 3.2.2. It is perhaps worth

Appendix A
Further results
Here we shortly resume further work, which was done by various authors
using the method of covering excess. Much of this work generalizes the set
F = {0, 1} to an alphabet consisting of q letters. Then Aq(n, d) denotes the
maximal cardinality of a q-ary code with minimum Hamming distance d and
Kq(n, R) the minimal cardinality of a q-ary code of length n and covering
radius R. Of special interest is the ternary case q = 3 due to its connection to
football pools. So for instance the determination of K3(n, 1) is widely known
as the football pool problem (for n matches): try to forecast the outcome of
n football matches, each having three possible results (including a tie). You
get a second price, if one of your forecasts has n − 1 correct results. Then
K3(n, 1) equals the least number of forecasts to place, which, no matter of
the outcome of the matches, guarantee at least a second price.
The method of covering excess is often combined with the use of linear
inequalities on the quantities Ai(x) deﬁned in (3.27).
A.1 Notes to Chapter 2 and 3
Generalizations of the binary Hamming codes (Theorem 1) to the q-ary case
can be found in every textbook on coding theory, see for instance Cohen et
al. [4], or in an early paper of Shapiro and Slotnick [28].
An improvement of the bound K(n, 1) ≥ 2n/n for even n (Theorem 3) is
due to Habsieger [9], if additionally n is divisible by 5, but not divisible by 3.
In all other cases (apart from a few sporadic values of n) Theorem 3 gives the
currently best known lower bound on K(n, 1) if n is even. A generalization
71
of Theorem 3 to lower-bound Kq(n, 1) if q and n are even, can be found in
van Wee [30].
A analogous approach to the theory of covering excess can be used to
upper-bound Aq(n, d), see van Wee [30].
A covering excess in spheres of radius two corresponding to Theorem
5 in the ternary case q = 3 and R = 1 (occurring if n ≡ 0 (mod 3) or
n ≡ 2 (mod 3)) is studied in Habsieger [8] and van Wee [30].
A.2 Notes to Chapter 4
Generalizations of Theorem 52 to the q-ary case can be found in Chen,
Honkala [3] and van Wee [30]. Lower-bounding K(n, R) with the use of
a covering excess in spheres with radius two is studied by Honkala [14] and
in the q-ary case by Chen, Honkala [3]. Lower bounds for Kq(n, R) in the
ternary case q = 3 can also be found in Habsieger [8].
A covering excess in spheres of radius two in the binary case, based on the
nonexistence of certain pair covering designs, was already investigated in the
early paper of Johnson [18]. The method was later rediscovered by Zhang
[32]. The corresponding ternary case q = 3 is studied in an unpublished
paper from Lo and Zhen [24].
Many of the currently best known lower bounds for K(n, R) result from
a similar covering excess in a sphere of radius three, see the papers Li, Chen
[21] and Lo, Zhang [23].
Application of the method of covering excess to binary/ternary mixed
codes are considered in van Wee [30] and especially in van Lint jr., van Wee
[22].
